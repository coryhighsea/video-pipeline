In Canada, registering a company is a fee and an online form. In Germany, my co-founder and I needed a notary, a court, three different registers and the tax office. We did it all in German, without a lawyer.

From start to finish it took **about four months and €606 in fees** before we could legally send an invoice, and eight months until our first paying customer.

Here's the overview: which company type to pick, the steps in order, what they cost, and what's different when you're not German.

## First: do you even need a UG?

Most side hustles don't. Germany has simpler options:

- **Freelancer (Freiberufler):** for "liberal professions" like developers, designers, writers and consultants. You register with the tax office, and that's it.
- **Sole proprietor (Einzelunternehmen):** for trades, like selling products. You register at the trade office (Gewerbeamt) and the tax office.
- **UG (haftungsbeschränkt):** a limited liability company, often called a "mini-GmbH". This is what we chose, because we wanted to protect our personal assets.

The difference is how much paperwork you sign up for. A freelancer needs a few of the steps below. A UG needs all sixteen.

## What a UG actually costs

The minimum starting capital for a UG is **€1**, but around **€1,000** is a sensible start. The starting capital isn't a fee: it stays in your business account. A full **GmbH** needs **€25,000**, at least half of it paid in at the start.

The catch with a UG is that **25% of each year's profit goes into a reserve** until you reach €25,000, at which point you can convert to a GmbH.

Our actual fees were:

| What | Cost |
|---|---|
| Notary | €273 |
| Commercial register (Handelsregister) at the court | €300 |
| Trade office (Gewerbeamt) | €33 |
| **Total** | **€606** |

Plus a tax consultant (Steuerberater) afterwards, which I'll get to.

## The steps, in order

1. **Pick your company type** (see above).
2. **Fill out the Musterprotokoll**, the standard founding template from the IHK (chamber of commerce). Names, dates of birth, and each founder's share. Free, and an evening's work.
3. **Book an online notary appointment.** We had one within days.
4. **Prepare your share of the starting capital.**
5. **Attend the notary meeting**, online through an ID app. Ours took 15 minutes.
6. **Open a business bank account** and deposit the starting capital. We used Qonto. You can open it while the company is still "in formation".
7. **Send the proof of deposit** back to the notary.
8. **Wait for the Handelsregister entry.** This was the slowest step, about four weeks.
9. **Pay the notary and court fees.**
10. **Register in the Transparenzregister**, the register of who owns the company. Free for us, and 15 minutes online.
11. **Register your trade at the Gewerbeamt.**
12. **Decide whether to use the VAT exemption** (more on this below).
13. **Send the tax questionnaire to the Finanzamt** through ELSTER, the online tax portal.
14. **Answer the Finanzamt's follow-up questions.** This took us weeks.
15. **Get your tax number** and send your first invoice.
16. **Find a tax consultant.**

## What's different as a foreigner

### You can do it in German, even with imperfect German

We asked for the notary appointment in English. It's possible, but it costs more, so we did it in German. We're both from Canada and have lived here for years: our German isn't perfect, but it's manageable, and that was enough for the notary, the trade office and the tax office.

### Your residence permit card works for the online notary

The online notary meeting needs an electronic ID through an app. If you aren't German, the electronic residence permit card (eAT) has the same online ID function as a German ID card.

### Check your residence permit first

This wasn't an issue for us, but it can be for others. If you're not an EU citizen, look at what your residence permit allows. If it doesn't allow self-employment (look for "Erwerbstätigkeit gestattet" or "selbständige Tätigkeit gestattet"), ask the immigration office (Ausländerbehörde) before you register a company.

### Tell your employer

If you're starting this next to a job, most German employment contracts require you to tell your employer about a side job (Nebentätigkeit). They can only refuse for good reasons, like direct competition. Tell them before you start.

## The VAT decision to make before you start

When you register with the tax office, you choose whether to be a **small business (Kleinunternehmer)**. Small businesses don't charge VAT and don't file VAT returns.

Since 2025 you qualify if your revenue was at most **€25,000 last year** and stays under **€100,000 this year**. If you opt out, you're **locked in for five years**.

We opted out, expecting either no revenue or enough to go over the limit quickly. Looking back, unless you already have customers lined up, **the exemption is probably the better choice**. We ended up filing quarterly VAT returns before we had any revenue.

## What I'd do differently

- **Start before you have customers.** Four months is a long time to keep a paying customer waiting for an invoice.
- **Keep the company description broad.** Ours was "Development, sales and delivery of software services", so we never had to go back to the notary when our plans changed.
- **Use the Handelsregister wait.** Following up doesn't speed it up. Get your ELSTER login and Gewerbeamt form ready instead.
- **Go to the Gewerbeamt in person.** The "online" version still needed a hand-signed paper sent by mail.
- **Register with the Finanzamt yourself.** Don't wait for the Gewerbeamt to pass your details on, and have your rental agreement ready if you use your home address.
- **Get a tax consultant early.** A company's yearly tax return isn't really a do-it-yourself job, and monthly bookkeeping makes it faster.
- **If you might apply for a startup grant, wait.** Some grants, like exist, require that you haven't founded the company or started selling yet.
