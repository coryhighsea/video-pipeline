## How it actually went

<p class="kicker">The story behind each step</p>

The process to register a company in Germany is a lot more complicated than in my home country of Canada. I've never registered a company in Canada, but from what I hear it's very easy: pay a fee online and that's it. In Germany I have registered a company, and it was a journey of its own.

Anyone can look up the official steps (or ask an AI). What nobody tells you is how it actually goes: which parts are easy, where you wait, what it costs, and the decisions you only understand afterwards. That's what this is.

### Step 1: Pick your company type

First you need to decide what kind of company: sole proprietor, partnership, freelancer or limited liability. We chose limited liability because we wanted the liability protection. In Germany that's called a **UG** (*Unternehmergesellschaft, haftungsbeschränkt*). It has a few more steps than a sole proprietor or freelancer, which are easier.

- [ ] Decide what kind of company you want to register

:::tip Employed? Read your contract first
If you're starting this next to a job, most German employment contracts require you to tell your employer about a side job (*Nebentätigkeit*). They can only refuse for good reasons, like direct competition, but tell them before you start.
:::

### Steps 2–5: The template and the notary

We were two people creating the company and decided on a **49% / 51%** split between me and my co-founder. The split matters because it decides how the starting capital is divided.

We found the *Musterprotokoll*, a template from the IHK (chamber of commerce), and filled in our names, dates of birth and our share of the starting capital.

- [ ] Fill out the template with your information and company details

So far: free, online and easy.

Then we needed a notary, and this is where it gets a bit more complicated. We submitted a request through an online notary platform and got an appointment relatively quickly. We asked for English, and they said it's possible but costs a bit more, so we did it in German. We're both from Canada and have lived in Germany for many years, so our German isn't perfect, but it's manageable. *We got this.*

The meeting itself was online too. You just set up a verification app connected to your ID to make sure everything is hunky dory.

- [ ] Book the meeting with the notary

In the meeting we presented the Musterprotokoll and a description of our company, which we kept very general: *"Development, sales and delivery of software services."* Then we signed with the notary. **The whole meeting took 15 minutes.**

- [ ] Attend the meeting with the notary

:::lesson Lesson learned
Doing the notary appointment in German saved money, and it was fine with "manageable" German. Keep your company description broad so you don't have to change it (and pay the notary again) when your business changes direction.
:::

### Steps 4, 6–7: Starting capital and the bank account

The minimum starting capital for a UG is **€1**, but it's recommended to start with around **€1,000** to cover at least the startup costs. A **GmbH**, the fully incorporated limited liability company, needs **€25,000** (at least half paid in at the start), and it looks more credible to clients.

The catch with a UG: **25% of each year's profit has to go into a reserve** until you reach €25,000, at which point you can convert to a GmbH.

The starting capital is yours to keep. You don't pay it to anyone. It just shows the company can cover its basic costs. Limited liability protects you personally, but the company itself still has to be able to pay if something goes wrong.

We chose €1,000, and I made sure I had my €490 ready for my 49%.

- [ ] Prepare your share of the starting capital

After the notary session we received paperwork to open the business bank account. We used **Qonto**, an online business bank that's popular with startups. You can open the account while the company is still a *UG in Gründung* (in formation). The bank gives you an IBAN, each founder transfers their share, and you send the proof back to the notary.

- [ ] Open the bank account and transfer the starting capital
- [ ] Send the proof back to the notary

### Steps 8–9: The waiting game

With the notarized registration and proof of the starting capital, the notary sends everything to the *Handelsregister*, the commercial register at the local court where you register.

**This took the longest: about four weeks.** I'd follow up and they'd say "no, no, we'll let you know."

- [ ] Wait

Then my co-founder received an invoice from the Handelsregister for about **€300**, and one from the notary for about the same. The business was registered and around €600 was paid, but we weren't done yet.

- [ ] Pay the court and notary fees

:::lesson Lesson learned
Following up with the Handelsregister doesn't speed it up. Use the waiting weeks to prepare the next steps: the Gewerbeamt form, your ELSTER login and your VAT decision.
:::

### Steps 10–11: The registers nobody tells you about

I had to enter our company and the ownership split in the **Transparenzregister**, online. It was free and easy for us. As far as I can tell it's there to fight money laundering. All I know is that it's required.

- [ ] Register in the Transparenzregister

Then we registered at the **Gewerbeamt** (trade office). This cost about **€50**. It can be done online, but in Germany it's almost easier to go to the local office in person, because the online version still ended up needing a hand-signed paper sent by mail.

- [ ] Register at the Gewerbeamt (UG, GmbH and sole proprietors; freelancers skip this)

### Steps 12–15: The tax office

Now… the tax office!

I read that the Gewerbeamt passes your details to the *Finanzamt* automatically, but it's faster to register with the tax office yourself. You do it through **ELSTER**, the online tax portal. I'd used it for my personal taxes already. It just needs a secure login with your ID.

The big decision here is whether to be a **small business** (*Kleinunternehmer*) and skip VAT. The upside: you don't charge VAT to your customers and you don't file VAT returns, so less of a headache.

But I'd heard it can get complicated. There were stories on YouTube of people who were under the limit in year one, went over in year two, had to switch to VAT, and ended up paying VAT on invoices where they never charged it. That can be a lot of money.

So we decided **not** to be VAT exempt, figuring we'd either make nothing or make a lot and go over the threshold quickly.

- [ ] Decide if you want to be a small business (VAT exempt) or not
- [ ] Submit the tax questionnaire to the Finanzamt (for freelancers, this is the main step)

The tax form asks for everything again: address, date of birth and so on. After we submitted it, the Finanzamt asked my co-founder for more paperwork: the rental agreement (our business address was his home address) and a few financial details. That back and forth added a few more weeks. **All in, it was about four months before we had our VAT number and could legally send invoices.**

The downside of not being VAT exempt: you file VAT returns even when you have zero revenue.

- [ ] Make sure you've received everything, then start sending invoices

:::warning The VAT rules changed in 2025
Since 1 January 2025 you can be a Kleinunternehmer if your revenue was at most **€25,000 last year** and stays under **€100,000 this year**. If you opt out of the exemption (like we did), you're **locked in for five years**. Check the current rules with a tax advisor before you decide.
:::

:::lesson Lesson learned
Looking back, unless you already have customers lined up and expect to go over the limit, **the VAT exemption is probably the better path**. We picked the "safe" option and got quarterly VAT returns before we had any revenue.
:::

### Step 16: After registration

We started all this early, before we had paying customers, because we knew it would take a while. We didn't want someone ready to pay us without being able to send them an invoice.

Bookkeeping starts right away. We were already paying for software and hosting, so we had expenses before any revenue. The VAT returns go to the Finanzamt through ELSTER.

**A tax consultant is pretty much necessary in Germany.** The yearly tax return for a company isn't really doable alone. It's possible from what I read, but not worth it. We found a *Steuerberater* who quoted us for the yearly taxes and offered monthly bookkeeping and VAT returns. Monthly bookkeeping also makes the yearly return faster.

- [ ] Find a tax consultant

Eight months after we started the whole process, we received our first payment from a real customer, and we got to live another day.

- [ ] Now the pain of running a business!

## Lessons learned, in one page

<p class="kicker">If you only read one page, read this one</p>

<ol class="lessons">
<li><strong>Start before you have customers.</strong> It took us about four months to be able to send an invoice. Don't let a paying customer wait on paperwork.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the simplest company type that fits.</strong> Freelancer or sole proprietor takes a few steps. A UG takes sixteen. Only go UG/GmbH if you really need the liability protection.</li>
<li><strong>Doing it in German is fine.</strong> Our German is "manageable" and that was enough for the notary and the offices. English is possible but costs more.</li>
<li><strong>Keep your company description broad.</strong> "Development, sales and delivery of software services" gave us room to change direction without going back to the notary.</li>
<li><strong>The starting capital isn't a fee.</strong> It stays in your business account. The real costs were about €650 in fees.</li>
<li><strong>The Handelsregister is slow and following up doesn't help.</strong> Use the wait to get ELSTER and the Gewerbeamt ready.</li>
<li><strong>Go to the Gewerbeamt in person.</strong> "Online" still meant mailing a signed paper.</li>
<li><strong>Register with the Finanzamt yourself</strong> instead of waiting for the Gewerbeamt to pass it on, and have your rental agreement ready if you use your home address.</li>
<li><strong>Think hard about the VAT exemption.</strong> With no customers yet, it's probably the better choice. Opting out locks you in for five years.</li>
<li><strong>Get a tax consultant early.</strong> Monthly bookkeeping makes the yearly return much easier.</li>
</ol>
